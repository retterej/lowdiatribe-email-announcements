Hi {{firstName}},

My 30-year career stalled. Not slowed — stalled. The options narrowed over months until one remained: take a job making pizzas at a regional chain in one of the lowest-income counties in Iowa. The kind of place that describes itself, without irony, as a pizza restaurant that happens to sell gas.

I took it as a bridge. A way to get from where I was to somewhere else. Something to fill the gap while the real thing materialized.

That's what you tell yourself. That this is temporary infrastructure. That you're just crossing.

---

I've read William Gibson since the 80s. His Bridge trilogy — people making a home out of infrastructure that was never meant for one — sat with me the way good speculative fiction does: not as prediction, but as a lens. A way of seeing systems that weren't built for people somehow becoming human anyway.

I found that this bridge was a Bridge.

Not a gap crossing — an ecosystem. Complete in its own logic, running on its own rules. The system has a way of finding what you know and putting it to work whether you offer it or not.

I'd estimate the system runs at about 25% of what it could be. Which sounds damning until you remember that's roughly the efficiency of an internal combustion engine — most of the energy going to heat, friction, noise, everything except forward motion. That's not a design flaw unique to this kitchen. That's the baseline condition of most systems. And like an engine, it runs.

That's not a failure story. That's a resilience story.

---

The thing I keep coming back to: it's a human-based system that isn't particularly human-focused.

It runs entirely on people — their judgment, their presence, their improvisation under pressure. Without the people, there is no system. And yet the system isn't built around the people mattering. Their experience, their dignity, their growth — these aren't load-bearing in the design.

The corporate apparatus has opinions about human-centeredness. They arrive as laminated posters and all-staff emails, addressed to everyone, written for no one in the room. The distance between the sentiment and the floor isn't cynicism — it's friction.

And yet the store isn't cold. People look out for each other, cover shifts, absorb the rough nights without complaint. The care is real — it just didn't come from the poster. It came from the people who showed up anyway.

I've built a career trying to create the opposite. Environments where talented people naturally do their best work. Systems that are human-based *and* human-focused. Being inside something that separates those two things so cleanly has been its own kind of education.

---

On my ninth shift, the kitchen manager asked if I wanted to be shift lead.

Nine shifts. Not nine months. The bar for leadership in that kitchen isn't tenure or credentials — it's showing up and being someone the system can lean on.

Competence recognizing competence. No announcement required.

---

The lessons from a pizza kitchen aren't lesser lessons because of where they came from. Lessons don't have a dress code. Systems don't care where you learned to read them.

I dropped to part time when the consulting contract came. I kept the option open deliberately — still there on weekends, still one of the people holding a piece of it together. Still living on the Bridge.

The Bridge didn't give me somewhere to get to. It gave me somewhere to be. And being there has shown me something that a single path — however well-built — never could have.
