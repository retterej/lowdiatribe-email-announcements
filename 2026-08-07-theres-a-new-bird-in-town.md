Valya didn't like the running tap.

She'd splash around happily in a sink full of water — curious, funny, completely herself — but the stream coming down made her nervous. I learned to run the faucet against the side of the sink, let the water pool quietly, and then step back. She'd hop in like she owned the place.

She was with us less than a year. One day she was fine. The next she was quiet and subdued. That night she was gone.

The bird who lived, then didn't.

---

There's a kind of grief that has a shape.

She was here. Now she isn't. That is sad. You can hold that. You can set it down, pick it back up, turn it over, know exactly what it is. The edges are defined. The loss is real and nameable — not easier, but *holdable*. You know what you're carrying.

The job search doesn't work that way. No edges. No date of loss to point to. Just the ongoing accumulation of not-knowing — the kind of thing you can't mourn because it hasn't ended, can't celebrate because it hasn't resolved, can't set down because it doesn't have a shape yet.

Valya's death landed in the middle of all of it. Small on its own. One more thing to carry.

Clean grief, I've learned, is easier to carry than the messy kind. At least you know what it is.

---

A few weeks after Valya died, one of our sons found a baby starling in a parking lot.

Valya had been a fresh hatchling when my wife found her — eyes still closed, entirely helpless, the whole world still ahead of her. This bird was maybe a week older. Eyes already open. Already scrapping. Already, somehow, insisting on surviving.

I hand-fed him for a few weeks. He forgot how to be fed and learned to eat on his own, just like Valya. He learned the rhythms of the house, just like Valya. He made himself at home in the particular way that starlings do — completely, immediately, without asking permission.

We named him Ajax.

He's a chatterbox — sings to himself, squawks out the window at whatever catches his attention. Ajax imprinted on me, which feels right, and also like something I didn't know I needed until it happened. When I walk into a room he lands on my shoulder before I've settled. He answers a cough with a squawk — not alarm, just response, like he's keeping his end of the conversation. He's not waiting to be invited. He already lives here.

He is not Valya. He doesn't try to be. I wouldn't want him to be.

---

When I prepared the sink for his first bath, I ran the faucet against the side the way I'd learned with Valya. Muscle memory. What the last bird taught me, carried forward into this one.

Ajax pecked at the side of the sink, trying to get under the flow.

So I moved the faucet to the middle. He stood directly beneath it — water pouring over him, splashing in every direction — with the particular joy that only a starling in a bath can project. Unbothered. Delighted. Completely certain this was exactly right.

Valya taught me how to run a bath for a starling. Ajax taught me that he wasn't Valya.

Both things are true at the same time.

---

The consulting engagement came through around the same time Ajax arrived. Four months — not the answer, not the destination, but a ray of light into a darkness that had been accumulating for a long time.

Ajax doesn't replace Valya. The consulting gig doesn't resolve the search. Neither of them tries to. That's not what they are.

What they are is simultaneous. Present in the same space as the loss, the uncertainty, the accumulating weight of not-knowing. Not after it. Not instead of it. Beside it.

The bird who lived taught me how to lead with curiosity.

The new bird in town is teaching me that joy doesn't need permission to arrive.

The water's in the middle now.
